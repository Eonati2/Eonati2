# Who to Target, and Who Signs

Two decisions, and they're different. **Which buildings** you pursue, and **which person** at those
buildings you write to. Getting the second one wrong wastes a perfectly good account.

---

# Part 1 — Which buildings

## Define your own limits first

Before any list, write down:

- **Radius.** How far will you actually send a crew, nightly, in traffic?
- **Building types** you can service.
- **Size floor.** The smallest monthly contract worth a walkthrough.
- **Size ceiling.** The largest job you could staff without breaking something.
- **Capabilities you don't have.** Medical waste, high-rise glass, floor restoration, union sites.

This takes ten minutes and saves weeks. Most wasted effort in commercial cleaning comes from
pursuing accounts the operator could never have serviced.

## Segment priority

In rough order of return, though your market may differ.

### 1. Property management — most work per conversation
One relationship can expose several buildings. Win the manager, not the building.

**Longer procurement** and they'll want insurance, references and responsiveness in writing. Worth
it: the second and third buildings come without a sales process.

### 2. Office and professional — the largest pool
Law firms, accountants, insurance, financial advisors, coworking. Straightforward scope, predictable
frequency, and they understand recurring contracts.

**Most contested.** Every cleaning company calls these. Your advantage is timing, not persistence.

### 3. Medical and dental — highest value per square foot
Frequency can't lapse. Compliance drives the specification. They pay more and they stay longer.

**Only if you're genuinely set up for it** — protocols, documentation, and any waste handling the
site requires. Getting in and failing here costs more than not getting in.

### 4. Industrial and light industrial
Larger contracts, more specialist scope, safety requirements. Good if you have the equipment.

### 5. Retail and everything else
Often has closing staff who already clean. Schools, childcare and gyms sit here: stable, less
contested, lower value, and often seasonal. Childcare in particular cares about background checks
more than price.

# Part 2 — Who signs

**A job title is not proof.** Confirm it before you treat someone as the decision maker.

| Building type | Who signs | Write to | Don't write to |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Property management** | Property Manager; Portfolio Manager if multi-site | The manager named on the building listing or the firm's team page | The leasing agent |
| **Office, under ~50 staff** | Office Manager | Office Manager | The managing partner |
| **Office, over ~50** | Facilities Manager | Facilities Manager | HR |
| **Medical / dental** | **Practice Manager or Office Manager** | Practice Manager | **The physician or dentist** |
| **Industrial** | Operations Manager or Plant Manager | Operations Manager | HR |
| **School / childcare** | Director, Administrator, or Business Manager | Director for a single site | Teachers |
| **Gym / fitness** | General Manager | GM | Front desk |
| **Retail** | Store Manager, only if not chain-decided | Store Manager | Corporate |

## The mistake that costs the most

**Writing to the dentist instead of the practice manager.** Medical is the highest-value segment
and the one where this error is most common. The dentist is treating patients, doesn't handle
vendors, and will delete it. The practice manager runs the building, holds the budget, and is the
person whose week gets easier if you're good.

Same pattern elsewhere: the managing partner doesn't buy cleaning, the office manager does.

## What three of them actually care about

**Property Manager.** Answers to building owners. Judged on tenant complaints and cost. *Worries
about:* a tenant escalating over their head, a vendor who doesn't answer the phone, being blamed.
*Wins with:* a written response-time commitment, and being able to cover more than one building.

**Practice Manager (medical).** Runs everything non-clinical. Compliance isn't optional. *Worries
about:* an inspection, a patient complaining about a restroom, clinical staff doing cleaning they
shouldn't. *Wins with:* documented protocols, consistent frequency, and reporting they can file.

**Office Manager.** Facilities is one of eight things they own. *Worries about:* chasing the
cleaners, the office looking bad when clients visit. *Wins with:* one point of contact and a
response time. They want it handled and invisible.

## Finding the name

Cheapest first. Stop when you have it.

1. **Their website** — about, team, leadership, contact pages. Best hit rate for this segment, and
   free.
2. **Google review replies** — managers and owners sign them by name. Consistently overlooked.
3. **Your state's business registry** — officers and registered agents. Free, public, reliable for
   smaller operators.
4. **LinkedIn** — company page, then people, filtered by role.
5. **Phone the front desk** — *"Who looks after the cleaning contract there?"* Often the fastest
   route, and people generally just tell you.

**Expect to find a name for roughly two-thirds.** If you're finding one every time, you're probably
recording guesses as facts.

## When you only have a generic inbox

`info@` isn't useless. It's useless when the email assumes it reached the owner.

Write it for the person who'll forward it: *"If this isn't your area, could you pass it to whoever
looks after the cleaning contract?"* Short, no assumptions, easy to hand on.

## Confirming someone actually signs

Mark them confirmed only when they've said so, or a published document says so. Until then, ask
early in the conversation:

> *"Besides yourself, who'd be involved in deciding?"*

Plain, normal, and it saves you a walkthrough for someone who can't buy.
