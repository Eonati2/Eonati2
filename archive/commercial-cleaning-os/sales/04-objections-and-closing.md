# 04 — Objections and Closing

**Instance B.** Stage 9 `Negotiation` → `Won` or `Lost`.

**The principle:** an objection is information, not resistance. Most are a request for a reason to
feel safe switching vendors. Answer the real question, do not argue, and never discount on the
first ask.

---

## 1. The nine objections

### "You're more expensive than the other quote"
The most common, and usually not about money — it is about not knowing what differs.

> *"That's fair — can I ask what their scope covers? Often the gap is frequency or supplies. If
> they're 3× weekly and we're nightly, that's the difference, and I can quote you a 3× option so
> you're comparing the same thing."*

Re-scope to match, or explain the difference in concrete terms. **Do not cut price to match a
different scope** — you will deliver the cheaper scope at the cheaper price and lose either the
margin or the account.

If genuinely like-for-like and you are 15%+ higher, either your cost model is off or you are not
their vendor. Both are worth knowing.

### "We're happy with our current cleaner"
Not a rejection. It is the normal state of a commercial building.

> *"Good — that's how it should be. I'd just ask to be the second call if anything changes. When's
> your contract up for renewal?"*

→ `Nurture`, review date set 60 days before renewal. **The renewal date is the single most valuable
field you can collect from a lost conversation.**

### "We need to think about it"
Usually means an unaddressed question or an absent decision-maker.

> *"Of course. Is there a part of the proposal you'd want more detail on — or is it more about
> timing?"*

Then: *"What would need to be true for this to be a yes?"* The answer is the actual objection.

### "Send me references"
A buying signal. Have three ready, in the same segment, with permission. Never invent one.

If you have none yet, say so and offer what you do have:

> *"I'll be straight with you — we're newer to medical offices specifically. I can give you two
> office references and I'd suggest a 60-day trial so you can judge the work rather than the
> reference."*

Honesty here outperforms a vague deflection, and it converts.

### "Can you do it cheaper?"
Never a flat yes. Trade something.

> *"I can get to that number — it'd mean moving to 4× weekly instead of nightly, or you supplying
> consumables. Which of those works better?"*

A concession without a trade teaches them the first number was padded, and it invites a second ask.

### "We're getting more quotes"
> *"Makes sense. Two things worth comparing: whether supplies are included, and what response time
> they commit to in writing. Those are where quotes usually differ. When are you deciding?"*

You have just given them a comparison framework built around your strengths — and it is genuinely
useful to them.

### "We're going to handle it in-house"
> *"That can work. The thing most places underestimate is coverage when someone's out sick. If
> that becomes an issue, I'm happy to quote a backup arrangement."*

→ `Nurture`, 90 days. In-house arrangements frequently break within a year.

### "Corporate decides this"
Qualification failure — should have surfaced in discovery.

> *"Understood. Is there someone at corporate I should be talking to, and would you be willing to
> pass my details along?"*

→ `Disqualified: chain_decides_centrally` unless they open the door.

### Silence after the proposal
Not an objection — a process failure. See `05-follow-up-cadence.md`.

## 2. Closing

Ask directly. Most cleaning contracts are lost to nobody asking, not to a competitor.

**After the proposal walkthrough call:**
> *"Does the Standard option look like the right fit, or would you want to adjust anything?"*

**When they are ready:**
> *"I can have the agreement over this afternoon and we'd start Monday the 15th. Does that work?"*

**When they are stalling but positive:**
> *"What's holding it up — is it the price, the scope, or is it just not the priority this month?"*

That question is uncomfortable and it is the most useful one in the sale. Any of the three answers
is actionable; silence is not.

**Never:** fake deadlines · invented discounts that expire · "I have another client interested in
that slot." Manufactured urgency in a small local market is remembered.

**Real constraints, stated plainly, are fine:** *"If we're starting the 15th I'd need to know by
Thursday to schedule the crew."* True, verifiable, and it works.

## 3. On a win

| Step | Detail |
|---|---|
| Signed agreement | Countersigned, both parties hold a copy |
| CRM | Stage 10 `Won`. Start date, monthly value, term, tier sold |
| Handoff | Owner → Ops. Written handoff with scope, access, contacts, special requirements |
| First-week check | Call after the first two cleans. **Do not wait for a complaint** |
| 30-day review | The first real inspection conversation |
| Reference | Ask at 90 days, not at 30 |

**The first two weeks decide retention.** A contract won and then quietly under-delivered costs more
than the one you lost, because it burns the reference too.

---

## 4. On a loss

Always ask, in one email:

> *"No problem at all — appreciate you letting me know. Would you mind telling me what the deciding
> factor was? It genuinely helps me."*

Most people answer. Log it as a code, not prose (`../crm/lifecycle-stages.md` §2).

| Pattern across losses | What it means |
|---|---|
| Mostly `price` | Cost model or targeting is wrong — you are bidding jobs you cannot win |
| Mostly `incumbent_retained` | Triggers are firing too late; you are arriving after the decision |
| Mostly `no_decision` | Discovery is not qualifying hard enough |
| Mostly `scope_mismatch` | ICP or serviceability scoring needs tightening |

**One loss is noise. Ten losses are a diagnosis** — and only if they were coded consistently.

Then: → `Nurture` with a review date, not `Lost` and forgotten. Losing a bid is the start of the
relationship with a building whose contract will come up again.
