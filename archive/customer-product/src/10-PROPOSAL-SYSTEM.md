# Proposal System

Sent within 48 hours of the walkthrough.

**What it's for:** making it easy to say yes, and making a price comparison harder than one number
against another. Most janitorial proposals are a price and a task list, which trains the buyer to
shop on price alone. Give them something else to compare.

Two to four pages. A twelve-page proposal doesn't read as thorough — it reads as padded.

---

## The seven sections

### 1. Cover
Their company, their building address, the date, your details. Nothing else.

### 2. What we understood
**Their words, back to them.** Three or four sentences from what they told you.

> *"You moved into the MacArthur building in August and need nightly service across roughly 4,200
> square feet, with particular attention to the restrooms and the entry glass. You mentioned the
> thing that would make you call is a restroom that hasn't been touched."*

**This is the most important section and almost nobody includes it.** It proves you listened, and
it frames everything after it against their problem instead of your service list.

Every sentence here must come from something they actually said. Nothing invented.

### 3. Scope of work
Specific and countable. Not *"restrooms cleaned and sanitized"* but:

> **Restrooms (2)** — nightly: fixtures cleaned and disinfected, mirrors, partitions spot-cleaned,
> floors mopped, dispensers restocked, waste removed.
> **Weekly:** partitions fully wiped, grout detail, vents dusted.

Break it out by area and frequency. If it isn't written here, it isn't in the contract — and that
protects both of you.

### 4. What's not included
Floor restoration, carpet extraction, window exteriors, post-construction, biohazard, day porter —
whichever apply, with a price if they want them added.

**Stating exclusions plainly builds more trust than leaving them out.** It also prevents the
argument in month three that ends the contract.

### 5. Three options

| | **Essential** | **Standard** ← recommended | **Complete** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Frequency | 3× weekly | 5× weekly | 5× weekly + day porter |
| Restrooms | 3× | Nightly | Nightly + midday check |
| Floor care | Quarterly buff | Quarterly buff + annual strip/wax | Monthly buff + 2× strip/wax |
| Glass | Monthly | Twice-weekly entry detail | Weekly, all interior |
| Supplies | You provide | **Included** | Included |
| Inspections | — | Monthly, with written report | Monthly + quarterly review |
| **Monthly** | $X | **$Y** | $Z |

**Mark the middle one recommended and say why in one line.** Three options change the question from
*"is this too expensive?"* to *"which one do I want?"* — a better question for both of you.

Price the tiers on real cost differences. Fake tiers built to make the middle look good are
obvious to anyone who's bought this before.

### 6. How it works
Start date · transition plan for the first two weeks · your supervisor's name and mobile ·
inspection cadence and what the report contains · how they raise an issue and your response time ·
insurance and bonding, certificate attached · background-check policy.

**The response-time commitment does more work than anything else on the page.** Most complaints
about cleaning companies are about responsiveness, not cleaning.

### 7. Terms
Monthly price and what triggers a review · term and notice period · payment terms · a
plain-English cancellation clause.

**Offer a 30-day out.** It costs almost nothing — someone who wants to leave will leave — and it
removes the biggest objection to switching vendors.

---

## Sending it

Email the PDF and say you'll call. Don't attach and vanish; don't phone without sending.

> *"Proposal attached for the MacArthur building. Three options — I've marked the one I'd recommend
> and why. I'll give you a call Thursday to walk through it, or reply any time if something's
> unclear."*

**Then actually call on Thursday.** That call is where the questions surface. Emailed proposals
nobody follows up on are how deals go quiet.

---

## Claims

Everything about them traces to what they told you. Everything about you is true.

| Never | Instead |
|---|---|
| "Your current cleaner is failing" | "You mentioned the restrooms have been inconsistent" — only if they said it |
| "100% satisfaction guaranteed" | State the actual remedy: response time, re-clean policy |
| Client logos you don't have permission for | Real references, with permission, or none |
| A statistic you can't source | Your own numbers, or nothing |
| "Award-winning" with no award | Leave it out |

In a market where owners talk to each other, an invented reference gets found out.

## The nine objections

**Structure:** acknowledge → clarify → isolate → respond → confirm the next step. Don't argue.

**"You're more expensive."** Usually not about money — it's not knowing what differs.
> *"Can I ask what their scope covers? Often the gap is frequency or supplies. If they're 3× weekly
> and we're nightly, that's the difference, and I can quote you a 3× option so you're comparing the
> same thing."*

Re-scope to match, or explain the difference. **Don't cut price to match a different scope.**

**"We're happy with our current cleaner."** Not a rejection.
> *"Good — that's how it should be. I'd just ask to be the second call if anything changes. When's
> your contract up for renewal?"*

**"We need to think about it."** Usually an unaddressed question or an absent decision maker.
> *"Of course. Is there part of the proposal you'd want more detail on, or is it more about timing?"*
Then: *"What would need to be true for this to be a yes?"*

**"Send me references."** A buying signal. Have three ready, same segment, with permission. If you
don't have any yet, say so and offer a trial period instead. Honesty outperforms deflection here.

**"Can you do it cheaper?"** Never a flat yes. Trade something.
> *"I can get to that number — it'd mean 4× weekly instead of nightly, or you supplying consumables.
> Which works better?"*

**"We're getting more quotes."**
> *"Makes sense. Two things worth comparing: whether supplies are included, and what response time
> they'll commit to in writing."*

**"We'll handle it in-house."**
> *"That can work. The thing most places underestimate is coverage when someone's out sick."*
Follow up in 90 days.

**"Corporate decides this."** A qualification failure. Ask for the contact, then move on.

**Silence.** Not an objection — a process failure. Five touches over about 17 days, then stop.

## Closing

Ask directly. Most cleaning contracts are lost to nobody asking.

> *"Does the Standard option look right, or would you want to adjust anything?"*

> *"I can have the agreement over this afternoon and we'd start Monday the 15th. Does that work?"*

When they're stalling but positive:
> *"What's holding it up — the price, the scope, or is it just not the priority this month?"*

That question is uncomfortable and it's the most useful one in the sale. Any of the three answers
is something you can act on. Silence isn't.

**Never:** fake deadlines, invented discounts that expire, "I have another client interested in
that slot." In a small local market, manufactured urgency is remembered.

**Real constraints are fine:** *"If we're starting the 15th I'd need to know by Thursday to
schedule the crew."* True, checkable, and it works.

---

## When you lose

Always ask, in one email:

> *"No problem at all — appreciate you letting me know. Would you mind telling me what the deciding
> factor was? It genuinely helps me."*

Most people answer. Record it as a category, not a paragraph: price, incumbent retained, awarded to
a competitor, no decision, scope mismatch, timing.

| Pattern across losses | What it means |
|---|---|
| Mostly price | Your cost model or your targeting is off |
| Mostly incumbent retained | Your triggers are too late — you're arriving after the decision |
| Mostly no decision | You're not qualifying hard enough before the walkthrough |
| Mostly scope mismatch | Tighten who you target |

**One loss is noise. Ten losses are a diagnosis** — and only if you recorded them the same way.

Then put them in your nurture list with a review date. Losing a bid is the start of a relationship
with a building whose contract will come up again.
